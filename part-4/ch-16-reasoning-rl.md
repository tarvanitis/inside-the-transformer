# Training Reasoning Models: GRPO and RLVR

The previous chapter trained models to match human *preferences*, to produce responses
that feel helpful, safe, and well-calibrated. That works well for conversational quality.
It fails for hard reasoning. Human annotators cannot reliably distinguish a correct multi-step
proof from a subtly wrong one. The preference signal degrades exactly where it matters most.

The approach that reshaped the field in 2024–2025 sidesteps this problem entirely: reward
the model not for matching preferences but for *getting the answer right*. On tasks with a
checkable answer (mathematics, code that must pass tests, logic puzzles) correctness is
cheap to verify and impossible to fake. This is **RLVR (Reinforcement Learning with Verifiable
Rewards)**, and it produces the "reasoning model" behavior now present in every frontier system.

**In this chapter**

- Why preference alignment cannot teach hard reasoning.
- The RLVR paradigm: verifiable rewards and their advantages.
- **GRPO.** The algorithm behind DeepSeek-R1 and most open reasoning models.
- The worked example: group-relative advantage on a simple arithmetic prompt.
- What emerges from verifiable reward training: chain-of-thought, backtracking, self-checking.
- Test-time compute scaling: the second scaling axis.
- Why distillation from a big reasoning model beats running RL on a small one.

This chapter assumes KL divergence from Chapter 2 and the PPO update structure from Chapter 15.

## Why preference learning hits a wall

The reward models in Chapter 15 learn from human comparisons. Human raters are reliable for
surface qualities (helpfulness, tone, length, format), but systematically unreliable for
correctness on tasks that require deep expertise. A rater comparing two proofs of a
combinatorics identity cannot tell whether either is correct. The winning response in a
preference comparison might be confidently wrong.

The consequence: RLHF makes models *persuasive*, not necessarily *correct*. On hard math,
code, and multi-step logic benchmarks, RLHF-aligned models plateau far below what the
underlying base model is capable of, because the preference signal is simply too weak where the
capability gap is largest.

::: {.callout .idea}
The fundamental limit of preference-based alignment is that annotators can only reliably
prefer what they can reliably evaluate. Verifiable reward training sidesteps the annotator
entirely: a Python interpreter, a math checker, or a logic verifier evaluates the response
with perfect precision and zero cost.
:::

## The RLVR paradigm

In RLVR, the reward signal is a **deterministic verifier**:

- **Mathematics**: check the final numerical answer against the known solution.
- **Code**: run the generated program and check whether it passes unit tests.
- **Formal logic**: verify proof steps mechanically.
- **Format constraints**: a regex checks whether the output contains properly formatted JSON.

RLVR works cleanly where correctness is checkable. Open-ended tasks (creative writing, nuanced
summarization) lack a hard verifier. Extending RLVR to those domains is an active research area
covered at the end of this chapter.

The verifier returns 1 (correct) or 0 (wrong). No learned reward model, no annotator, no
proxy, just a hard correctness check. This eliminates reward hacking at the source: the
model cannot "fool" a Python interpreter into thinking broken code passes tests.

::: {.callout .plain}
A *chain of thought* is just the model writing out its working before committing to an answer, the
way you would scribble on paper rather than do long division in your head. Because every token the
model produces is fed back in as context for the next one, those intermediate steps are not
decoration. They are extra computation. A model that writes "let me first find the common
denominator" has literally given itself more room to think.
:::

### The landmark results

**OpenAI o1** (September 2024) was the first widely deployed reasoning model trained this way,
demonstrating that extended chains of thought (generated by RL on verifiable rewards)
dramatically improved performance on competition mathematics and complex coding tasks.

**DeepSeek-R1** (January 2025) showed openly that the core recipe is surprisingly simple:
start from a strong base model, run large-scale RL where the reward is a verifier, and the
model spontaneously learns to think step by step, backtrack, and self-verify. The paper showed
that pure RL from a base model (**R1-Zero**) could develop coherent reasoning with no SFT at
all, though the resulting text was unpolished, mixing languages and formatting inconsistently. The
shipped **R1** added a brief cold-start SFT phase (Chapter 14) before RL to fix readability.

## GRPO: Group Relative Policy Optimization

The dominant algorithm for RLVR training is **GRPO**, introduced in DeepSeek's DeepSeekMath
paper (2024) and central to the R1 training recipe. Its key innovation over PPO (Chapter 15):
**replace the learned value network (the critic) with the group's own statistics**.

### The algorithm

For each training prompt x:

1. **Sample a group** of G responses from the current policy: y₁, y₂, …, yG.
2. **Score each** with the verifier: rᵢ ∈ {0, 1} (or a richer signal, e.g. partial credit).
3. **Compute group-relative advantages**: standardize the rewards within the group.
4. **Apply the clipped PPO update** using those advantages.
5. **Add a KL penalty** to keep the policy near the reference model.

::: {.callout .plain}
The whole trick is in step 3. PPO needed a second network, the critic, to guess how good each
response would be (Chapter 15's weather forecaster). GRPO throws that away. It generates a handful of
answers to the same question and grades each one on a curve against the others in its group: better
than the group average is a path to repeat, worse is one to avoid. The group's own average is the
yardstick, so no separate critic ever has to be trained.
:::

::: {.figure}
![](assets/figures/ch16/fig-grpo-baseline.svg)
:::

::: {.caption}
**Figure 16.1.** GRPO drops the critic. PPO trains a separate value network to estimate a baseline for each response. GRPO instead samples a group of responses to the same prompt and uses their average reward as the baseline, so no critic is needed.
:::

The full objective:

$$\hat{A}_i = \frac{r_i - \text{mean}(r_1 \ldots r_G)}{\text{std}(r_1 \ldots r_G)} \qquad \text{(group-relative advantage)}$$

$$L_{\text{GRPO}} = -\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{G}\sum_i \min\!\left(\rho_i \hat{A}_i,\; \text{clip}(\rho_i, 1{-}\varepsilon, 1{+}\varepsilon)\cdot\hat{A}_i\right)\right] + \beta \cdot D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_\theta \| \pi_{\text{ref}})$$

where ρᵢ = π_θ(yᵢ|x) / π_θ_old(yᵢ|x) is the importance ratio between the current and old policy.

Every symbol:

- **G.** Group size: how many responses are sampled per prompt (typically 8–64).
- **rᵢ.** Verifier reward for response i (often binary correct/incorrect).
- **mean / std.** Population mean and standard deviation *within the group*. This is the baseline
  that replaces PPO's separate critic network.
- **Âᵢ.** Group-relative advantage: how much better than the group average response i was,
  in standard-deviation units. Positive = above average (reinforce), negative = below (suppress).
- **ρᵢ.** Importance ratio. clip(ρᵢ, 1−ε, 1+ε) caps how much any single update can move the
  policy, the same stability trick as PPO.
- **β · D_KL.** The KL leash keeping the policy near the reference model. In some R1-style
  runs this is set to near zero or dropped entirely.

### Worked example: arithmetic verification

Prompt: "Compute 17 × 24." Sample a group of G = 4 responses. The verifier checks the final
answer (correct = 1, wrong = 0):

```
// Group rollouts and verifier rewards (17 × 24 = 408)
y₁ → "Let me compute: 17 × 24 = 17 × 20 + 17 × 4 = 340 + 68 = 408"   r₁ = 1 ✓
y₂ → "17 × 24 = 408"   r₂ = 1 ✓
y₃ → "17 × 24 ≈ 420 ... = 418"   r₃ = 0 ✗
y₄ → "24 + 24 × ... = 388"   r₄ = 0 ✗

// Group statistics (population std: divide by G, not G−1)
mean = (1 + 1 + 0 + 0) / 4 = 0.5
std  = sqrt([(1−0.5)² + (1−0.5)² + (0−0.5)² + (0−0.5)²] / 4) = 0.5

// Group-relative advantages  Âᵢ = (rᵢ − mean) / std
Â₁ = (1 − 0.5) / 0.5 = +1.0   ← reinforce y₁'s reasoning path
Â₂ = (1 − 0.5) / 0.5 = +1.0   ← reinforce y₂'s reasoning path
Â₃ = (0 − 0.5) / 0.5 = −1.0   ← suppress y₃'s reasoning path
Â₄ = (0 − 0.5) / 0.5 = −1.0   ← suppress y₄'s reasoning path

// No critic, no learned reward model — the baseline is the group mean.
// Over thousands of prompts the policy shifts toward reasoning paths
// that consistently reach verified-correct answers.
```

Half the group got the right answer, so the group mean is 0.5 and population std is 0.5.
Standardizing turns correct attempts into advantage +1.0 and wrong ones into −1.0. Nothing
here is a learned score that could be gamed. The reward is a hard arithmetic check.

![](assets/figures/ch16/fig-group-advantage.svg)
:::

::: {.caption}
**Figure 16.2.** Group-relative advantage. The rewards in a group are standardized against the group's own mean, so responses above average get a positive advantage (reinforce) and those below get a negative one (suppress).
:::

::: {.callout .note}
**The edge case: all correct or all wrong.** When every response in the group gets the same
reward, std = 0 and the advantage is undefined (division by zero). In practice, GRPO
implementations add a small δ to the denominator, or skip the update for that prompt (ε is already
the clip range in the loss above). This
situation also signals that the prompt is either trivially easy (all correct) or well beyond
the current policy (all wrong), and both are informative.
:::

### GRPO vs PPO

| Property | PPO (RLHF) | GRPO (RLVR) |
|---|---|---|
| Value network (critic) | Yes, a separate trained model | No, group mean is the baseline |
| Reward source | Learned reward model (proxy) | Verifier / rule (ground truth) |
| Models in memory | 4 (policy, reference, RM, value) | 2 (policy + reference), plus the π_θ_old snapshot when rollouts are reused. The verifier is a deterministic function, not a model |
| Reward hacking risk | Higher, proxy can be exploited | Lower, verifier is deterministic |
| Main use today | Preference alignment | Reasoning, code, agentic tasks |
| Representative models | GPT-4, Claude 2 | DeepSeek-R1, o1/o3, Qwen3 |

::: {.caption}
**Table 16.1.** GRPO against PPO: what removing the critic changes.
:::

## What the model learns

The surprising result from R1 and its successors is that models trained with RLVR develop
reasoning behaviors that were *never explicitly demonstrated in training data*:

- **Step-by-step decomposition.** Breaking hard problems into subtasks.
- **Backtracking.** Recognizing a dead end mid-chain and trying a different approach.
- **Self-verification.** Checking an intermediate result before proceeding.
- **Extended thinking.** Allocating more tokens to harder problems.
- **Honest uncertainty.** "let me re-check this" rather than committing to a wrong answer.

These behaviors emerge from one feedback signal: binary correct/incorrect on thousands of
math and code problems. No human wrote "first, check whether your calculation is right." The
model discovered that self-checking correlates with getting the right answer.

::: {.callout .lens}
**Mechanistic.** The model is not "thinking" in any deep sense — it is learning that certain
token sequences (a scratchpad-style chain of thought ending in a correct answer) receive
reward 1, and others do not. The emergent behavior is real and useful, but its origin is
statistical: patterns that correlate with verifiable correctness get reinforced.
:::

## Test-time compute scaling

Reasoning models expose a second scaling axis beyond model parameters and training tokens:
*inference-time compute*. Allowing the model to generate a longer chain of thought before
committing to an answer (or sampling multiple attempts and selecting the best) consistently
raises accuracy on hard tasks.

::: {.callout .deepdive}
**Best-of-N and process reward models.** The simplest test-time scaling strategy: sample N
responses, score each with a verifier or PRM (process reward model, Chapter 15), and return
the highest-scoring one. (The *N* here is a sample count, not the layer depth *N* of Chapter 9.) This "best-of-N" approach is embarrassingly parallelizable (N
independent rollouts) and shows substantial gains on math benchmarks. At N = 100, even a
small model can match a much larger one at N = 1. OpenAI o1 and o3 reports, and DeepSeek-R1's
open analysis, both show accuracy curves that keep rising as inference budget grows. This
reframes evaluation: a model's score now depends on how much compute it is *allowed* at
inference, not just on its weights.
:::

> **Current reasoning models (as of 2026-09-14).** Every major lab now ships a reasoning ("thinking")
> mode trained on this approach: OpenAI's GPT-5.6 / GPT-6, Anthropic's Claude 5, Google's Gemini 3,
> DeepSeek, Qwen, and others. Chapter 1 and Appendix B (Table B.6) carry the full dated roster. The
> durable shift is that "allocate more tokens to hard problems" is now a trained-in capability across
> the frontier.

## Distillation beats small-model RL

One of the most practically important findings from the R1 work: **distilling** a big
reasoning model into a small one via SFT (Chapter 14) consistently outperforms running GRPO
on the small model directly.

The mechanism (generate ~800k chain-of-thought traces from the big model, then fine-tune the small
one on them with standard SFT) is covered in Chapter 14. What matters here is the finding: the small
model acquires the teacher's step-by-step reasoning, backtracking, and self-verification far more
cheaply than it could rediscover them through RL. This is why capable open reasoning models
proliferated through 2025: once DeepSeek-R1's traces were public, any lab could fine-tune a Llama or
Qwen base on them.

::: {.callout .caution}
**Distillation limits.** A distilled small model inherits the reasoning *style* of the teacher
but not its full capability ceiling. It can follow the teacher's patterns on problems similar
to the training examples, but may struggle with genuinely novel problem types the teacher would
handle by exploration. For frontier reasoning on new tasks, running RL directly remains
necessary. Distillation is a shortcut to a good starting point, not a replacement for full
GRPO training.
:::

## Beyond verifiable domains

The RLVR recipe works cleanly for math, code, and formal logic: tasks with a deterministic
correct answer. Extending it to open-ended domains (creative writing, knowledge-heavy Q&A,
multi-turn dialogue) is an active research frontier:

- **Generative reward models (LLM-as-judge)** can score open-ended responses, but reintroduce
  the reward-hacking risk that verifiers eliminate.
- **Rubric-based grading**: a detailed rubric (with specific criteria and point allocations)
  gives a structured reward signal for tasks without a single correct answer.
- **Process reward models (PRMs)**: scoring intermediate reasoning steps rather than only the
  final answer, enabling denser feedback on long chains of thought.

The field has not yet settled on a clear winner for open-ended RLVR, making this one of the
most active research areas in alignment as of 2026.

## Summary

- **RLVR** trains models on tasks with checkable answers, replacing a learned reward model with
  a deterministic verifier. This sharply reduces reward hacking and produces signals that are precise
  exactly where preference alignment is weakest.
- **GRPO** is the dominant algorithm: sample a group of G responses, compute group-relative
  advantages (no value network needed), apply the clipped PPO update. Two models in memory (three
  if the old-policy snapshot is kept) versus PPO's four.
- **Emergent reasoning** (chain-of-thought, backtracking, self-verification) arises from
  the correctness signal alone, not from imitation of human reasoning demonstrations.
- **Test-time scaling** gives reasoning models a second performance axis: more inference
  tokens consistently raises accuracy on hard tasks.
- **Distillation** (SFT on traces from a large reasoning model) is cheaper and often more
  effective than running GRPO on a small model from scratch.
